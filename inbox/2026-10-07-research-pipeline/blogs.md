## Summary

1. The practitioner blogs agree on one order of work: first the reason (who pays you and why, or who is forced to trade), then the cheapest test that could disprove it, and only then a costed backtest. Sinclair, Longmore, Eifert and Abdelmessih all say this; Chan and Quantpedia start closer to published anomalies.
2. Hypotheses come from forced or price-insensitive flows, known risk premia, floor-trader anecdotes, forgotten assumptions, and relative-value measurement of vol (IV against realised, term structure, skew, earnings-stripped vol). Public trading content, LLM-found patterns and data-first scanning are warned against.
3. The best worked options example is Longmore's: "vol realised under implied, so be short vega" becomes a test of whether expensiveness persists, run on VIX against SPY realised vol — not a backtest.
4. Notebooks: Robot Wealth runs the whole study in hosted notebooks, on shared auto-updated data, in GitHub, reviewed by pull request; the simulator lives in a package. Sinclair argues the opposite: stay manual (legal pad, spreadsheet) as long as you can.
5. Options backtest conventions from sellers (ORATS, spintwig): fill at a quote 14 minutes before the close, pay 53–75% of the spread, enter every day to average out timing luck, discount results by 20%, and list every assumption.
6. Self-deception checks: data is "in sample forever" once seen (Sinclair); explicit, implicit and tacit fitting (Carver); neighbour-parameter checks (ORATS); shifted roll dates (Hoffstein, 400 bp of tracking error); stress tests of unobservable assumptions (SpotGamma); review in a small group (SIG, Lebron).
7. Live: paper "incubation" for 6–12 months (Davey), yearly live-against-backtest cost reconciliation (Carver), passive small orders near theoretical value (Eifert). The views on when to stop conflict.
8. Records: Carver's outcome-sorted research scorecard (7 of 17 studies implemented), Abdelmessih's "beliefs graveyard", Robot Wealth's Edge Database, and one firm account of a one-week kill rule.
9. Disagreements kept open below: correcting for the number of trials, building walk-forward frameworks, when costs enter, when to stop.
10. Nothing on research process found from OptionMetrics, Cboe or Predicting Alpha blogs; Lebron's own writing was not found (only quoted by Moontower).

## 1. Where hypotheses come from

**Sinclair: idea first, data second.** Euan Sinclair says you cannot start from data, because there is too much to test without an idea from somewhere else. He browses SSRN, follows citations through Google Scholar, and warns that targeted search keeps you inside what you already know. https://robotwealth.com/finding-edges/ (2025-08-31). "There is too much stuff to test until you have an idea from somewhere else." `[named practitioner]` — posted on Robot Wealth, which sells the RW Pro membership.

**Sinclair: floor traders notice first.** He treats anecdotes from market makers and floor traders as evidence: they see patterns before statistics do. The best case is a mismatch between theory and what a trader observes. Same URL. "I can tell you stuff that you can go and nerdify." `[named practitioner]`

**Sinclair: forgotten assumptions.** A third source is assumptions that people no longer see as assumptions. His example: nobody prices an American call and put at the same strike with different volatilities, though early exercise makes them differ under a steep term structure. Same URL. "no one uses different volatilities for calls and puts of the same strike." `[named practitioner]`

**ORATS: screen vol statistics against three benchmarks — peers, a forecast, own history.** ORATS builds screening ratios for options ideas: 30-day ex-earnings IV (earnings removed), skew slope, contango (slope of ATM ex-earnings IVs out to 45 days), and forward against "flat forward" vol. Each is compared three ways: against a weighted average of the stock's sector-ETF components (ratio against its 10-day average), against ORATS's own forecast, and against its one-year percentile. It warns that skew slope alone "is not very predictive"; only its change against recent levels is. It reports that extremes in the flat-forward/forward ratio came before large stock moves (Oct 2017, Feb 2018). https://orats.com/university/predictive-indicators (undated). "Alone, it's not a very predictive, because it's just a reflection of how the IVs look" `[seller]`

**Sinclair (2026): begin with effects you already believe — carry, momentum, risk premia.** In a podcast that Kris Abdelmessih quotes at length on Moontower, Sinclair says a beginner should start from a few well-known effects with thousands of SSRN pages behind them. The concept that makes a trade work matters more than the instrument. He says the variance premium is real but "slippery", and a bad place to start. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "The actual instrument and sector are of lesser importance than the concept" `[named practitioner]`

**Sinclair: discomfort points to edge.** You are paid for something unpleasant, such as risk insurance. If a trade looks dangerous to a professional, most of the world agrees, and someone gets paid to carry that fear. His example: who has the edge, the seller or the buyer of options over the weekend? Same URL. "if it makes you nervous, there's probably edge in there somewhere." `[named practitioner]`

**Sinclair: is it an inefficiency or a risk premium?** Price competition drives an inefficiency to zero, so you exploit it hard while it lasts. A risk premium lasts because the buyer has a different utility function — the buyer wants the insurance. You can build a trading life only on the second kind. Same URL. "is this an inefficiency that people can't access, or is it genuinely a thing everyone knows" `[named practitioner]`

**Abdelmessih: measure and rank, do not predict.** His one prediction is "over time, cheap stuff outperforms expensive stuff"; the work is measuring. In options that means normalising vols, time (volatility time), funding and any other driver of price across markets, so that cheap and expensive "are just colors on a heatmap". He asks a manager which metrics they watch, why they matter, and how they know they still matter. https://moontower.ai/blog/measurement-not-prediction (2020-12-01). "Investment edge is about measurement not prediction." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` (moontower.ai)

**Sinclair: there are few true sources of edge, and they move across markets.** In Amberdata's written summary of his podcast, Sinclair says edge is a real phenomenon (trend, mean reversion, carry, spreads, risk premia), and mathematics only measures it. Quants err by starting from models. An edge understood in one market can often be carried to another, which is why traditional-finance edges appeared later in crypto. https://blog.amberdata.io/euan-sinclair-on-volatility-risk-management-and-trading-psychology (2026-01-13; vendor summary, not a transcript). "quants often make the mistake of starting with models" `[seller]` — Amberdata sells crypto derivatives data; Sinclair advises it.

**Eifert: find the participant who cannot be picky, and trade only when they dislocate a price.** In a podcast transcript on Mutiny Fund's site, Benn Eifert describes his trade ideas through who is forced to trade: a big hedger buying front-month VIX calls near 50 cents every month whatever the strike; banks hedging structured notes, which distorts dividend futures; heavy retail inflows into VIX products. Most of the time each of QVR's strategies has no position, because no dislocation exists. https://mutinyfund.com/benn-eifert-qvr-advisors/ (Episode 20, undated page, c. 2020). "We don't believe in general and derivatives markets that there's ever a trade you should just have on all the time" `[named practitioner]`

**Abdelmessih and Lebron: watch a thin order book.** Abdelmessih recalls Agustin Lebron saying he got ideas from staring at the order book for hours, in medium- or low-liquidity names. Abdelmessih adds a corollary: edge falls as liquidity rises, so in SPY whatever is left looks like a risk premium. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "edge is inversely related to liquidity." `[named practitioner]` — Abdelmessih sells moontower.ai, an options analytics tool.

**Longmore: look for price-insensitive, forced trading.** He says a pattern (mean reversion, trend) is not an edge, only a label. The edge is the reason, usually someone forced to trade without regard to price: a margin call, a leveraged token rebalancing at a fixed time, 60/40 managers rebalancing near month end. Three patterns that look alike in a scan have three different reasons. He trusts flow-based edges more than trend, because he can name who trades, why and when. https://robotwealth.com/to-trend-or-not-to-trend-wrong-question/ (2026-04-10). "Mean reversion, trend, momentum: these aren't edges. They're labels for how prices move." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` `[general, not options]`

**Longmore warns against public trading content, much academic work, and LLM-found patterns.** He calls most public trading content "absolute rubbish" (patterns, signals, backtests) and says many academic papers exist to show off mathematics rather than explain markets. An LLM trained on this corpus finds patterns and runs backtests, but skips the question "who's losing money on the other side, and why will they keep doing it?". Each pass of hypothesise–test–learn–iterate compounds understanding; letting the LLM do it does not. https://robotwealth.com/more-of-the-disease-faster-what-happens-when-you-ask-an-llm-to-find-you-an-edge/ (2026-03-19). "who's losing money on the other side of this, and why will they keep doing it?" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Quantpedia: screen a database of published anomalies, then judge them on post-publication data.** Quantpedia's own workflow starts with a filter over its database of strategies taken from papers. It then switches from "paper metrics" to out-of-sample metrics, keeping candidates with stable Sharpe, controlled drawdowns and clear economic intuition. Its loop: Screen → Validate → Combine → Compare → Diagnose → Refine. https://quantpedia.com/quantpedias-research-workflow-from-idea-discovery-to-portfolio-construction/ (2026-03-23, David Mesíček). "Many academic strategies look compelling in sample." `[seller]` — sells Quantpedia Premium/Pro subscriptions. `[general, not options]`

**Chan: build on a known principle, not on data mining; then try small changes.** For a strategy taken from someone else, Chan says: replicate every detail, check for backtest pitfalls, then try to improve it "by small changes". Models should rest on well-researched phenomena (his examples: cointegration, post-earnings drift). https://epchan.com/img/links/Backtesting-and-its-Pitfalls.pdf (c. 2012–13). "Try not to use a pure data-driven/data-mining approach." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` `[general, not options]` — Chan's "improve by small changes" is the step that Sinclair calls overfitting (sections 6 and 8).

**Sinclair: academic papers give the observation, not the trade.** You read dozens to find one useful paper, and usually only the basic observation helps. Academic ideas also often fail the "can you actually trade it" test (retail access, OTC). https://robotwealth.com/finding-edges/ (2025-08-31). "You will need to read dozens to find anything useful" `[named practitioner]`

## 2. From idea to testable statement

**Sinclair: a one-sentence reason, pitched like a start-up.** Before any test, the idea needs a one-sentence reason why it might work, given as an elevator pitch to yourself. A structural reason passes; a model description does not. Then ask others for feedback. https://robotwealth.com/finding-edges/ (2025-08-31). "“I provide liquidity when markets are distressed” could be convincing." `[named practitioner]`

**Sinclair: say where the effect must appear and where it must not.** His worked hypothesis: 60/40 funds rebalancing at month end must buy bonds after stocks outperform. Test that the effect appears where the reason predicts, and is absent where the reason predicts absence; if it also shows up in the second week of the month, the reason is wrong. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "do you not see it in situations where your hypothesis tells you you should not see it?" `[named practitioner]`

**Abdelmessih: a trade idea takes the form "this, because that".** His basic decision is relative: "I'm buying this for X, because that is Y bid" — for example, one-month AAPL straddles against three-month QQQ puts. A decision that loses that grammar has become "the line went down". He says he and most professional vol traders had no edge in timing the level of vol. https://medium.com/@moontower/2-trading-concepts-to-get-ba-396386d746a4 (2024-12-15). "Traders are searching for contradiction not prediction" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` (moontower.ai)

**Longmore: a three-sentence edge pitch, and the order of work.** The pitch: (1) what causes the inefficiency — who trades, why, and why they ignore price; (2) why you can exploit it — why someone faster has not taken it all; (3) how you would harness it. Screening first and adding a reason afterwards is data mining; writing the reason and what the data should show, then looking, is research. Noticing something in the data first is allowed, but the "why" must be answered before trading. https://robotwealth.com/to-trend-or-not-to-trend-wrong-question/ (2026-04-10). "It depends entirely on the order you do things." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Longmore: an options example worked from idea to first test — "vol realised under implied, so be short vega".** In his case study, excerpted on Moontower, he takes that trader's line apart. It hides two assumptions: that realised below implied means options were expensive, and that expensive options stay expensive next period (persistence, or autocorrelation), which "you must look for". He does not backtest a short-vol strategy, because a backtest carries arbitrary choices, luck and path dependence. He measures the persistence directly with the cheapest proxy data: VIX against monthly realised vol from SPY returns, instead of 30-day ATM SPX implied vol. His rules: question hidden assumptions; ask what you would see if the idea were true and if it were false; use the simplest data that could disprove it. https://moontower.substack.com/p/case-study-on-becoming-a-partner (2024-11-13). "A lot of beginners, including me, would try to do a backtest ... That's nearly always a bad idea." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Longmore: ask "what sucks about this edge?"** Ask why you, and not a bigger, faster firm, can take the edge. If the answer is "because I'm smarter", there is no trade. Edges left for small traders have something unattractive about them: very noisy, capacity-limited, margin-heavy, with a horrible skew profile, or operationally awkward. Same URL. "The reason that you or I can participate in an edge is that there's something that sucks about it." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**spintwig: numbered theses, fixed KPIs, and a mirror thesis, written before the results.** Each spintwig options study opens with numbered theses. Example: short SPX 45-DTE put verticals opened only on days its s1 signal says "overpriced" will beat daily entry on four named KPIs (total return, Sharpe, max drawdown, drawdown duration). The mirror thesis says entry on "underpriced" days will do worse than daily entry. Daily entry is the baseline. The verdict reads "Thesis supported: 4 of 4 KPIs". https://spintwig.com/short-spx-vertical-put-45-dte-s1-signal-options-backtest (2024-08-30). "This study seeks to explore the following theses" `[seller]` — sells trade logs, consultations and a signal, and earns ORATS affiliate fees.

**Longmore: "who pays you and why" comes before any data.** Kris Longmore's four steps are: edge first (who pays you and why), try to disprove it with simple data analysis, size it at portfolio level, repeat. He learned it from mentors in professional low-latency futures trading. https://edgealchemy.robotwealth.com/p/edge-alchemy-for-independent-traders (2025-09-03). "Edge first (who pays you and why)" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` — Robot Wealth sells the RW Pro membership and courses.

## 3. Data

**ORATS: what a vendor's options history contains, and its size.** Near-end-of-day chains, with Greeks, theoretical values and IVs, go back to 2007, taken 14 minutes before the close. One-minute chains start in August 2020 and are about 28 TB, growing about 21 GB per trading day, so ORATS ships them on hard drives. A two-minute archive covers 2015 to September 2020. https://orats.com/university/historical-data (undated, current). "roughly 28 TB and growing about 21 GB per trading day" `[seller]` — sells this data.

**Abdelmessih: take event variance out before comparing implied vols.** Before comparing one name's vol with another's, or an index with its parts, remove the earnings day from the term structure. Variances add, so term variance = regular variance + event variance. His example: 40 days to expiry, 36% IV, 24% "regular" vol, so the earnings day is a 171% annualised event (about 10.7% daily, an 8.6% expected straddle move), carrying about 57% of all variance to expiry. He calls this normalising grind "measurement", and supplies a spreadsheet (behind the paid section). https://moontower.substack.com/p/how-an-option-trader-extracts-earnings (2025-01-23; free part read, rest paywalled). "Normalizing data so that the comparisons are relevant is so much of the work to be done." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**spintwig: a short-dated study cannot start before the product existed.** Its methodology page lists the dates each weekly expiry was listed: SPX Friday weeklies on 2005-10-28, Wednesday on 2016-02-23, Monday on 2016-08-15, Tuesday and Thursday in September 2022 (SPY's on other dates). Any 0–7 DTE study must start no earlier than these. It also notes that commissions were 4.95–19.99 USD per trade from the late 1990s to the early 2010s, so a flat modern commission flatters the early years. https://spintwig.com/methodology (published 2019-03-22, updated 2026-09-23). "most trade commissions ranged between 4.95 USD to 19.99 USD in the late 1990s through early 2010s" `[seller]`

**ORATS: index "spot" is a forward implied from put-call parity.** Index options price off futures, and not every expiry has a future. ORATS therefore solves put-call parity for an implied future at each expiry and reports that as the "stock price". It warns that its index prices will not match other sources for this reason. Dividend forecasts are also tempered when the option market does not confirm them. https://orats.com/university/historical-data (undated). "ORATS uses put-call parity to solve for the futures at each expiration." `[seller]`

## 4. Notebooks

**Robot Wealth: the whole study lives in a hosted notebook, shared on GitHub, reviewed by pull request.** Kris Longmore and James Hodges run research in Jupyter notebooks on Google Colab, so every member opens the same clean Ubuntu instance. Each study sits in a GitHub repository; a shared R package pulls cleaned data; a snippet library stops people rewriting analysis code; members submit research by pull request for review. Reproducibility is their first rule. https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/robot-wealth-part-twocollaborative-research-for-systematic-trading/ (2024-02-05, interview by Burt Shulman). "When you start researching a potential edge, it's all very iterative and messy." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` — RW Pro membership.

**Sinclair argues against early code: work by hand as long as you can.** He says to do things "as manually as possible" — a legal pad, then a spreadsheet — and to automate only for operational and key-man risk. Each step away from the market removes a chance to learn; systematising probably makes a strategy worse because it locks it in. This is the strongest anti-tooling view found in this lane. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "Every time you're removing yourself from interacting with the market, you're removing yourself from an opportunity to learn." `[named practitioner]`

**Li (2011): the backtest takes the longest, and skipped details split paper from real P&L.** Haksun Li says the research path from idea to execution takes months, and the backtest is the slowest part: data cleaning, simulation, slippage, calibration, sensitivity and debugging. Most people "approximate" these details, which is one main reason paper and real P&L differ. He also notes that academic strategies are hard to reproduce because most authors publish no code. https://nmfin.tech/2011/10/27/the-role-of-technology-in-quantitative-trading-research/ (2011-10-27). "This is one major reason why real and paper p&l's are different." `[seller]` — sells the AlgoQuant research platform. `[general, not options]`

**Robot Wealth "Lab": themed research pods, notebooks tied to auto-updated data.** Longmore's case study adds detail to the setup above. Notebooks run on a hosted Jupyter service connected to curated datasets that update automatically, so anyone can re-run a study with new data later. Research is grouped into themed "Research Pods" (for example a crypto pod). A strategy that goes live gets a dashboard tracking its factors and weights, served by API to members. https://moontower.substack.com/p/case-study-on-becoming-a-partner (2024-11-13). "The Lab is all about facilitating a scalable, reproducible research effort." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Longmore: four kinds of work — wear one "hat" at a time.** He splits research into four jobs with different tools: (1) edge research, the curious scientist — is the effect real and why, with costs and sizing ignored; (2) strategy research, the engineer — backtests, realistic costs, constraints; (3) portfolio research, the architect — combining and sizing; (4) operations — today's orders, positions, reconciliation. The most common error is blurring 1 and 2, optimising entries for an effect not yet understood. The test before entering stage 2: can you explain the effect to someone in two minutes? https://edgealchemy.robotwealth.com/p/everything-everywhere-all-at-once (2026-02-21). "The research phase is where you earn the right to build." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Disagreement — when do costs enter?** Longmore says to leave costs out of edge research ("it can wait until the science is done"). Chan (section 6) makes cost assumptions part of the screen before any backtest, at least for high-frequency ideas. Sinclair's view, already in the vault (`research/_raw/backtest-and-data.md`), is that a daily-hedged straddle's hedging cost can be worth about two vol points — so for delta-hedged options ideas, costs may decide the verdict.

**Robot Wealth: what moves out of the notebook — the simulator.** Their open R package `rsims` takes the "natural outputs of the quant research process": a matrix of prices and a matrix of target weights. The notebook does the research and produces target weights; the package applies trade buffers and commission and runs the backtest. Its README says the cost model covers only commission, borrow and funding, not spread or impact. Public example notebooks sit in `Robot-Wealth/r-quant-recipes` (research-examples, a UVXY spreadsheet, SPX constituents). https://github.com/Robot-Wealth/rsims (README; last push 2026-09-29). "Function inputs should be close to the natural outputs of the quant research process" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` `[general, not options]`

**Quant Galore: public options repos split by stage — dataset builder, backtest, simulation, production.** The pseudonymous author of "The Quant's Playbook" newsletter publishes one repo per study. The SPX credit-spread repo `at-the-money` holds `fade-backtest.py`, `fade-backtest-sim.py`, `fade-production.py` and a hedging script. The `arimax-options` repo pairs `SP500_Training.ipynb` with `SP500_Production.ipynb` and separate dataset-builder and backtest scripts. The notebook is used for fitting, and scripts for the repeatable steps. https://github.com/quantgalore/at-the-money and https://github.com/quantgalore/arimax-options (pushed 2024-11 and 2023-10). (file names, no prose quote) `[unknown]` — pseudonymous; the newsletter has a paid tier.

**Carver: one engine for backtest and live; the system is a config file.** Carver runs his automated futures system on his open-source pysystemtrade, the same code he uses to backtest. The whole system is a public YAML config plus a short run script. https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2021/12/my-trading-system.html (2021-12). "my open source backtesting and trading engine, pysystemtrade" `[named practitioner]` `[general, not options]`

**Longmore: AI writes the notebook code; the human supplies the idea and judges the result.** He uses AI for boilerplate, data wrangling, charts and tests every day, but says it cannot answer "who pays you and why". His working split: human generates the theory of edge, AI implements, human evaluates. https://robotwealth.com/brave-new-backtest/ (2026-03-29). "AI for the implementation, humans for the thinking." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

## 5. The backtest

**Sinclair: hedge to isolate the edge, not for comfort; long backtests age.** In the same Amberdata summary, good hedging removes exposures you do not want, while bad hedging reshapes the return distribution to feel better and destroys the edge. He adds that structural change (the IBIT bitcoin ETF, index concentration) makes long historical backtests less relevant. https://blog.amberdata.io/euan-sinclair-on-volatility-risk-management-and-trading-psychology (2026-01-13; vendor summary). "Hedging for comfort, rather than survival, is a recurring mistake." `[seller]`

**ORATS: fill at a quote 14 minutes before the close, and pay most of the spread.** ORATS says the closing option price is a poor value; quote quality holds until about 14 minutes before the close, so its backtester trades there. It charges slippage of 75% of the bid-ask width for a single leg, falling to 53% for a four-leg spread (multi-leg orders travel less past mid). https://orats.com/university/backtesting-methodology (page undated; backtester launched 2023). "We use a slippage of 75% of bid ask width for single legs" `[seller]` — sells options data, a backtester and APIs.

**Longmore: compare signals frictionless first; the backtest comes later.** In his stat-arb series he plots cumulative returns of several candidate signals with no costs, and labels the plot as not a backtest. The aim is a rough comparison of turnover, decay and concentration before any costed simulation. https://robotwealth.com/when-is-a-mispricing-not-a-mispricing/ (2026-06-01). "This isn't a backtest… There are no costs or frictions." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` `[general, not options]`

**spintwig: write every simplification down.** Its mechanics list states the trade time (3:46 pm ET), commission per contract, margin rule per structure, and a slippage table. It also lists what it assumes away: no early assignment, ample liquidity at all times, and starting capital "set using hindsight bias" so margin calls never happen. https://spintwig.com/short-spx-vertical-put-45-dte-s1-signal-options-backtest (2024-08-30). "Margin calls never occur (starting capital is set using hindsight bias" `[seller]`

**spintwig: cut the reported result by 20%, and set DTE windows to the expiry calendar.** It tells readers to discount every reported result by 20% (a 10% CAGR becomes 8%) for what the model leaves out: one daily snapshot at 3:46 pm, hindsight-chosen capital, static Reg-T margin, no early assignment, unlimited liquidity. Slippage is a fraction of the spread: buy at bid + (ask − bid) × s, where s = 0.5 is mid and 1.0 is the market maker's price. DTE targets get tolerance windows set by the expiry calendar (45 DTE means 28–62, so a position can open every day around quarterly expiries). Positions that would expire after the end date are dropped. https://spintwig.com/methodology (updated 2026-09-23). "it is suggested to discount depicted performance by 20%" `[seller]`

**ORATS: enter every qualifying day to remove path dependence; report notional returns.** Its backtester opens one trade each day the entry rule is met, so many overlapping trades run at once, because a start date can change results. It reports return on notional, not on margin, so strategies compare fairly, and warns that broker margin rarely measures true risk (a short put needs 20% under portfolio margin, 100% in a cash account). https://orats.com/university/backtesting-methodology (undated). "starting a backtest on the first day of the year might have very different results" `[seller]`

## 6. Checks against fooling yourself

**Sinclair: once you have looked at data, it is in-sample forever.** He says most people now backtest wrongly: they cross-validate, walk forward, go out of sample, and still find things because they looked at so many things. Trying mean reversion on the S&P because trend following failed there is already overfitting. His options example: a strangle sold Monday fails, a straddle sold Tuesday works — both are the same variance-premium play, so the second result is luck. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "as soon as you've looked at some data once, you're done. That data is in sample forever." `[named practitioner]`

**ORATS: check the neighbours of the best parameter.** Testing ten expiries and keeping the best one is overfitting. Look at similar backtests instead: if inputs that are close give very different results, that is a bad sign. Its tool has a "find similar" button for this. https://orats.com/university/backtesting-methodology (undated). "Backtests with similar inputs but a wide variety of results is a bad sign." `[seller]`

**Eifert: hedge ratios fitted on recent data fail out of sample.** A hedge ratio estimated at high frequency and sensitive to recent data "tends to not do very well out of sample"; QVR keeps trade construction and hedge ratios fairly stable while positions change. https://mutinyfund.com/benn-eifert-qvr-advisors/ (c. 2020). "it just tends to not do very well out of sample" `[named practitioner]`

**SpotGamma: stress the assumption you cannot observe.** Dealer gamma models assume dealers are short every call and long every put. After a critic attacked this, SpotGamma kept 50–90% of the assumption and randomised the rest of the open interest as long or short, 1,000 trials per step. At 50% the total SPX gamma fell to 44% of the model's figure. They argue the signal still holds because its effect "tops out" above about $1bn of gamma, and they admit a deeper study is needed. This is a quick spreadsheet test of an identifying assumption, run before defending a signal. https://spotgamma.com/spx-gamma-model-positioning-adjustments/ (2020-02-10). "Certainly this cannot be 100% true, but the back data ... is statistically compelling" `[seller]` — sells gamma-exposure data and dashboards.

**Hoffstein: run the same option strategy on shifted roll dates.** Newfound Research replicated a self-financing three-month put-spread collar three times, changing only the roll schedule. Any two versions differed by more than 400 basis points a year of tracking error. This "rebalance timing luck" hides inside any options backtest with one fixed roll date. https://blog.thinknewfound.com/2023/01/the-hidden-cost-in-costless-put-spread-collars-rebalance-timing-luck/ (2023-01-24; paper on SSRN 4336419). "the annualized tracking error between any two implementations is in excess of 400 basis points" `[named practitioner]` — Newfound manages funds.

**Chan: screen a published strategy before you backtest it.** Ernest Chan's talk slides give a filter to apply before any backtest. Skip a strategy with high returns but Sharpe below 1 and a two-year drawdown, because it is probably a fluke. Doubt high-frequency results until you know the cost and order-type assumptions. Reject any stock result not run on the universe as it existed then. His cheap look-ahead test: check whether the program can make a position on the last bar. His rule of thumb: each extra optimised parameter needs another year of daily data. https://epchan.com/img/links/Backtesting-and-its-Pitfalls.pdf (undated slides, c. 2012–13). "every additional parameter to be optimized requires an extra year of daily data." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` — runs workshops and consulting. `[general, not options]`

**Disagreement — build a walk-forward framework, or not?** Longmore says not to build complicated walk-forward frameworks early: split the data into subsets and see how different the factor plots look, and solve only the problems you have now. Davey and Carver (both below) treat formal walk-forward as a core step. https://moontower.substack.com/p/case-study-on-becoming-a-partner (2024-11-13). "don't set up complicated walk-forward frameworks. Just split your data into subsets" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Davey: one shot at out-of-sample, on data kept back.** He tries early ideas on small subsets of the data, so the rest stays clean for validation. The walk-forward test is run once and not adjusted, because tweaking after the result is implicit overfitting. https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/036-kevin-davey-part-i-its-all-about (2025-04-17). "Kevin insists on a one-shot test" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` `[general, not options]`

**Carver: three kinds of overfitting — explicit, implicit, tacit.** Explicit fitting (an optimiser picks parameters) can be done honestly: few degrees of freedom, no time machine (expanding or rolling window), and robust weighting across variations. Implicit fitting is any choice made after you see the results, including a single failed test you then forget. Tacit fitting is knowledge from before the test, such as "momentum works", that the backtest could not have known in its first year. https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-three-kinds-of-overfitting.html (2021-09-02). "you should know when you are doing implicit fitting" `[named practitioner]` `[general, not options]`

**Disagreement — correct for the number of trials, or not?** Sinclair says to count every failed test (below). Carver does not like correcting the significance level for the number of trials: the correction treats a major parameter cheat the same as a small tweak. He prefers a "fund-level" backtest that holds every idea ever tried, or tests on many paths (CPCV, combinatorial purged cross-validation). Same Carver URL. "correcting your significance level for the number of trials ... which I don't like" `[named practitioner]` `[general, not options]`

**Review by colleagues: SIG's norm, and Lebron's small group.** Abdelmessih says at SIG it was normal, whenever you replayed a decision, to point out where you were prone to bias. Todd Simkin (SIG) adds that people see others' errors, such as anchoring to last week's vol, far more easily than their own. SIG's language of "how many cents of edge are in a trade" forced estimates to be pinned down. Agustin Lebron advises a trusted, mixed group of about 4–8 people with agreed rules for signalling confidence; a large Discord or Twitter is no substitute. https://moontowermeta.com/trading-is-a-team-sport/ (2022-10-15). "the only way cognitive bias inoculation works is via group reinforcement" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` (moontower.ai)

**Sinclair: count the failures, and grade belief from 0 to 10.** Ask how many other things were tried and failed, and include them. He grades how far he believes an effect: candlestick charts 0, carry 10, "gold goes up on Fridays" 3, whatever its t-statistic. He sizes positions by this degree of belief first, then runs the statistics. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "how many other things have you looked at that didn't work? Because you've got to include those." `[named practitioner]`

**Longmore: a backtest is not research.** He argues that backtesting, statistics and pattern matching are not research; the mechanism is. He lists "validate with out of sample data" among the conventional wisdom that LLMs and the internet repeat — a deliberate provocation against the standard checklist, not a rejection of testing (his own step two is "try to disprove it"). https://robotwealth.com/brave-new-backtest/ (2026-03-29). "backtesting, statistics, and pattern matching aren't research." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` — compare Carver, Chan and Davey above, who treat out-of-sample testing as a core step.

## 7. From a passed test to real money

**Eifert: enter by resting small random orders near theoretical value, and learn from the fills.** QVR does not ask a broker for a price. It keeps orders in the market, for example bidding no more than 0.05 vol points below theoretical mid, in random batches of one to ten lots. Had they bought everything offered on the first day, the price would have dropped another 10 points afterwards; the fills tell them someone is selling. https://mutinyfund.com/benn-eifert-qvr-advisors/ (c. 2020). "no more than 0.05 volatility points below theoretical mid-market ... batch sizes between one and 10 lots" `[named practitioner]`

**Carver: a yearly live-against-backtest reconciliation, cost line by cost line.** Each year he publishes his live results next to the backtest. In year two: Sharpe a little over 1.0 live against 0.88 in the backtest; total costs 1.39% against 1.5% backtested; slippage 0.63% against 1.0% (helped by his execution algorithm); commissions 0.76% against 0.5%. He found the backtest left out futures rolls, about a third of commissions, and planned to add them. Realised vol was 24.6% against a 25% target. He warns that even a few years of live results cannot separate skill from luck; in the comments he notes that a faster system gives evidence sooner only if its Sharpe is higher. https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2016/04/futures-trading-performance-year-two.html (2016-04-07). "even a few years worth of live trading performance may not tell you with much statistical significance" `[named practitioner]` `[general, not options]`

**Longmore: the mechanism decides what to do in a drawdown.** If you know the mechanism, a drawdown raises answerable questions: has the counterparty adapted, has the structural constraint gone? Those answers tell you whether to sit tight, adapt, or walk away. A pattern with no mechanism leaves only anxiety and more tinkering. https://robotwealth.com/more-of-the-disease-faster-what-happens-when-you-ask-an-llm-to-find-you-an-edge/ (2026-03-19). "Has the structural constraint been removed?" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Vogel (Alpha Architect): write down the bad decade before you start.** Jack Vogel shows that strategies his firm believes in can lag a free benchmark for a long time: value and momentum deciles behind the S&P 500 for 2008–2017, a 12-month trend rule behind buy-and-hold for 2010–2017. His advice: set the goal, build a process with a logic, ask honestly "can I stick with it?", and describe the bad scenario in advance, in a calm setting, so that a long lag does not force a change at the worst time. https://alphaarchitect.com/trust-the-process/ (2018-06-21). "Sometimes the investment process will not work over long periods" `[named practitioner]` — Alpha Architect manages funds. `[general, not options]`

**Disagreement — when to stop.** Vogel says sit through long lags if the logic still holds. Davey (below) swaps out weak strategies every month from a library. Longmore (above) says to check whether the mechanism — the counterparty or the constraint — has changed. Carver says a few years of live data cannot separate skill from luck.

**Davey: 6–12 months of "incubation" without money, then a library and monthly rotation.** In the interviewer's written summary, Kevin Davey (futures, over 30 years) sets a pass mark first (a return-to-drawdown ratio), runs one walk-forward test with no changes afterwards, then a Monte Carlo resample of trades. Survivors are watched live without real money for 6 to 12 months, which exposes slippage, commissions and data problems. Validated strategies join a library of over 200; about 20 trade at once, and each month weak performers are swapped out. https://algoadvantage.substack.com/p/036-kevin-davey-part-i-its-all-about (2025-04-17, Simon M). "monitored in a live market environment—without real money—for 6 to 12 months" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]` — Davey sells courses; Algo Advantage sells courses. `[general, not options]`

**Sinclair: trade by hand first, to learn execution.** Manual trading teaches adverse selection and execution, which a backtest and an automated system hide. His example: there seems to be plenty of volume, but each time he shows a 100-lot the bids disappear. https://moontower.substack.com/p/hard-earned-trading-wisdom (2026-04-29). "every time I put a 100 lot in, all the bids disappear." `[named practitioner]`

## 8. Records and killing ideas

**Abdelmessih: a notebook of beliefs with a "graveyard".** He keeps a notebook of beliefs in three groups: current priors, a beliefs graveyard (examples: "profit margins should mean revert", "more risk equals more reward"), and evergreen beliefs, which he publishes on a wiki. Killed beliefs are kept and labelled, not deleted. https://moontower.ai/blog/measurement-not-prediction (2020-12-01). "I maintain a notebook of these beliefs." `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Carver: a public scorecard of three years of research, sorted by outcome.** When he rebuilt his futures system, Carver listed his studies from about three years in four groups: "knew wouldn't work, but someone asked" (4); "hoped would work, but didn't" (2); "worked, but I decided not to implement", because too complex or too simple (4); and "worked and I did implement" (7). Each item links to its blog post, so the blog is his research log. Some items are rejected for complexity even though they worked. https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2021/12/my-trading-system.html (2021-12, updated 2022-01-05). "Things I hoped would work, but didn't" `[named practitioner]` `[general, not options]`

**Longmore: edges are retired, and the reason is recorded.** He lists edges traded with the community that have since gone — FX strategies, VX futures calendars, straddles over earnings, post-earnings drift, a meta-labelling equity strategy — and gives two reasons for retiring them: they seemed to die, or the team was too busy trading other things. https://moontower.substack.com/p/case-study-on-becoming-a-partner (2024-11-13). "some we've retired because they seemed to die, others because we got too busy" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**spintwig: one methodology page with a dated change log, and a fixed trade-log format.** All studies point to one methodology page, which keeps a dated change log (for example 2025-06-30, "section defining types of margin calls"). Every study's KPIs are computed from a CSV trade log with fixed columns: entry date, leg, strike, DTE, entry price, delta, IV rank, exit date and price, expiry price, profit after fees. https://spintwig.com/methodology (updated 2026-09-23). "All strategy key performance indicators (KPIs) are derived directly from the trade logs." `[seller]`

**How long one idea takes, at a firm: weeks, with a one-week kill rule.** A newsletter that compiles anonymous practitioner accounts quotes an HFT researcher at a London firm. A project usually takes a few weeks, plus a couple more to reach production; many are killed within a week; most researchers run two projects at once to avoid dead time. The same researcher says choosing what to work on is harder than finding ideas. The piece also mentions a fund's weekly meeting that reviews live performance against backtests, and a "spike checker" that flags any daily move over 20% so corporate actions are handled before models break. https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/inside-the-quant-researcher-trader (2026-05-12; free part read). "if I don't have a good angle within a week it gets put in the backlog" `[unknown]` — anonymous accounts compiled by a paid newsletter. `[general, not options]`

**Robot Wealth: an "Edge Database".** All ideas waiting for research, and summaries of every study the group has done, sit in one shared database so no one repeats work. The screenshot shows a status board per study. https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/robot-wealth-part-twocollaborative-research-for-systematic-trading/ (2024-02-05). "we maintain an Edge Database where we store all our ideas for future research" `[named practitioner]` `[seller]`

**Carver: a failed idea stays in the record.** Trying one idea, seeing it fail, and forgetting it is implicit fitting. His fix is a fund-level backtest that stores every idea ever considered and gives each a risk allocation from the start, so the account curve is not inflated by only showing the survivors. https://qoppac.blogspot.com/2015/11/the-three-kinds-of-overfitting.html (2021-09-02). "a 'fund level' backtest in which every single idea we've ever considered is stored" `[named practitioner]` `[general, not options]`

**Sinclair: kill ideas fast; tweaking does not rescue them.** If an idea does not clearly point to money, no tweaking will make it robust; you will overfit or fail even at that. Discarding a good idea too early is less likely than wasting time on a bad one. https://robotwealth.com/finding-edges/ (2025-08-31). "Once you have an idea, be prepared to give up on it quickly." `[named practitioner]`
